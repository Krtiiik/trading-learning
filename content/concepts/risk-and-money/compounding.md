Compounding means that your gains also earn gains. When you leave a gain invested, the next year's return is on a bigger sum, so your money grows by more each year.

## Example

Example, not a forecast: you invest $10,000, and it grows 7% every year.

- Year 1: $10,000 earns $700. You have $10,700.
- Year 2: $10,700 earns $749. The extra $49 is the first year's $700 earning 7% too.

| After | Gains left in | Start + gains taken out |
|-------|---------------|-------------------------|
| 10 years | about $19,700 | $17,000 |
| 20 years | about $38,700 | $24,000 |
| 30 years | about $76,000 | $31,000 |

If you take the $700 gain out each year, you have $10,000 + 30 × $700 = $31,000 in all, not $76,000.

Most of the growth comes late: the first 10 years add about $9,700, the last 10 about $37,000. So time matters most: 30 years instead of 20 gives about $76,000 instead of $38,700, about twice as much.

A quick check is the **rule of 72**. Divide 72 by the yearly return in percent to get about how many years your money takes to double: at 7%, about 10 years.

## Dividends

A [[dividend]] that you take out as cash stops compounding. A reinvested one buys more shares, which pay dividends too. An accumulating [[etf]] reinvests them for you; a distributing one pays them out. Tax on fund payouts differs by EU country. In Czechia, a distributing fund's payouts are taxed each year, so less money stays invested. An accumulating fund is not taxed until you sell, and not at all after 3 years ([[taxes-on-trading]]).

## Costs compound too

A yearly fee comes off the return every year, and the money it takes stops earning too. The same $10,000 for 30 years, with the fee simply taken from the 7%:

| Yearly fee | After 30 years |
|------------|----------------|
| none | about $76,000 |
| 0.07% | about $74,600 |
| 0.75% | about $61,600 |

At the start, the 0.75% fund costs only $75 a year on $10,000, but it ends about $13,000 behind the 0.07% fund. This is why [[buy-and-hold]] investors look hard at a fund's expense ratio (in Europe, TER) and at other [[fees-and-commissions]]. Taxes and the costs of frequent trading work the same way.

## Losses compound too

Returns multiply; they do not add. A 10% rise and then a 10% fall turn $100 into $110 and then $99, not $100. A 50% fall needs a 100% gain to recover: $10,000 falls to $5,000, and $5,000 must double ([[drawdown]]). So [[volatility|big swings]] cost growth: the +10% and −10% have a simple average of 0%, yet you lost $1. Of two investments with the same simple average of yearly returns, the one that swings less ends with more.

## Watch out

- **The 7% is only an example.** Actual returns vary, and some years are losses.
- **Inflation compounds too.** At 2% a year, prices rise about 80% in 30 years, so $76,000 then buys only about what $42,000 buys now.
- **Money you take out stops compounding.** Selling in a fall locks in the loss and can miss a recovery.
- **Your currency changes the result.** In koruna or euros, a dollar result also moves with the exchange rate ([[currency-risk]]).
