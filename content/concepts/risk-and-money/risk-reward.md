Risk/reward compares the money you could gain on a trade with the money you could lose. You find it before you buy. The risk is the distance from your entry (the price you buy at) to your [[stop-loss]]. The reward is the distance from your entry to your **target**, the price where you plan to sell for a gain.

## How to find it

Divide the reward by the risk:

**ratio = (target − entry) / (entry − stop)**

Despite the name, traders usually put the reward first: "3 to 1" or "3:1" means a possible gain of three times the possible loss. Many sources and apps write the risk first (1:3), so check which way a number is written.

## Example

You have a $10,000 account and risk 1%. You buy 25 shares of a [[stock]] at $42 with a stop at $38, a size set by [[position-sizing]]. Your target is $54.

- Risk: $42 − $38 = $4 per share, $100 for the 25 shares.
- Reward: $54 − $42 = $12 per share, $300 for the 25 shares.
- Ratio: $12 / $4 = **3 to 1**.

The price passes $54 and still rises, so you decide to keep the shares and sell later at $64: a gain of $22 per share. After the fact, the trade made 5.5 times the risk (5.5 to 1). Keep the plan (3 to 1) and the result apart.

## The win rate you need

Your win rate is the share of your trades that end in a gain. If every trade ends at its stop or at its target, the ratio tells you how often you must win to break even (no gain, no loss), before costs:

**break-even win rate = 1 / (1 + ratio)**

| Ratio | Wins needed to break even |
|---|---|
| 1 to 1 | 50% |
| 2 to 1 | 33% |
| 3 to 1 | 25% |

At 2 to 1, one win pays for two losses, so winning more than a third of your trades makes money. Real exits often differ from the plan, as the $64 sale shows. For what a [[strategy]] makes per trade, see [[win-rate-and-expectancy]].

## Costs make the ratio smaller

[[fees-and-commissions|Fees]] and the [[spread]] (the gap between the best price buyers offer and the best price sellers want) come off every gain and add to every loss. With $5 of fees for the round trip (buying, then selling), the example gives $295 of possible gain against $105 of possible loss: about 2.8 to 1, not 3 to 1.

## Watch out

- **A far target looks good but is seldom reached.** A target at $82 gives 10 to 1 on paper. If the price almost never gets there, the ratio is only a hope.
- **Do not move the stop to make the ratio look better.** A stop at $40 instead of $38 turns the same target into 6 to 1. But the stop belongs where normal day-to-day wobbles do not reach it; too close, and a wobble triggers it and sells you out of a trade that would have worked.
- **The ratio means nothing alone.** Trades planned at 3 to 1 that win 20% of the time lose money.
