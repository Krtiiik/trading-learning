Currency risk is the chance that exchange rates make an investment worth more or less in your own money. It comes with every asset whose value depends on another currency, even when the asset's price does not move.

This site's examples are in US dollars. If your money is in Czech koruna (CZK) or euros (EUR), you change a dollar result back at that day's exchange rate. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, written as a [[forex-pair]] such as USD/CZK (koruna per dollar).

## Example

A dollar costs 21 CZK. You change 210,000 CZK into $10,000 of an S&P 500 [[etf]] (a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock).

- **The fund rises, the dollar falls.** The fund gains 10%, to $11,000. The dollar falls 10%, to 18.90 CZK. You have 11,000 × 18.90 = 207,900 CZK: about 1% less.
- **The fund stays flat, the dollar rises.** The dollar rises 10%, to 23.10 CZK. You have 10,000 × 23.10 = 231,000 CZK: a 10% gain.

A 10% gain multiplies your money by 1.10 and a 10% fall by 0.90, so together: 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99.

## What counts is what the fund holds

An ETF that follows the MSCI World (an index of large companies in many rich countries), bought on Xetra, a German [[exchange]], is priced in euros. But over 70% of the fund is US companies. If the dollar falls against the euro, the fund's euro price falls by about 70% of that move.

The [[fees-and-commissions|currency conversion fee]] your [[broker]] takes, often 0.25% to 1.5% each way, is a separate cost.

## Currency-hedged funds

Some ETFs have a **hedged** version, often marked "EUR Hedged", which uses contracts to remove most of the exchange-rate effect. You pay a slightly higher expense ratio (the fund's yearly fee), and the contracts cost about the gap between the two currencies' short-term interest rates. With US rates higher, a euro-hedged US stock fund gives up about 1 to 2 percentage points a year (as of 2026; this changes with interest rates). Only a handful hedge to CZK; euro hedging leaves the smaller EUR/CZK risk.

## Months and decades

Over months, a currency move can be as large as the stock move: in the first half of 2025, the dollar lost over 10% against the koruna and the euro. Over decades, stock growth is usually much larger: [[buy-and-hold|compounding]] at 7% a year makes $10,000 about $76,000 in 30 years, still about six times your money after a 20% dollar fall. Currency matters more for short trades, and for [[bond|bonds]] (loans to governments or companies) with their smaller returns.

Holding assets in several currencies is part of [[diversification]], but even world funds are about two-thirds to three-quarters in dollars.

## Watch out

- **Currency moves can be large.** A dollar cost about 40 koruna in mid-2001 and under 15 in mid-2008: a fall of more than 60% in seven years.
- **[[dividend|Dividends]] come in dollars too.** Their koruna value moves with the rate.
