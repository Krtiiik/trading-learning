Diversification is spreading your money over assets that do not all move the same way. Then one bad loss takes only a small part of your account.

## Two kinds of risk

- **Company-specific risk**: bad news that hits one company, such as a failed product or a lost court case. Only that [[stock]] falls.
- **Market-wide risk**: news that hits almost all companies at once, such as a recession or a sudden rise in interest rates. Most stocks fall together.

Diversification removes most of the first kind, not the second. The S&P 500, an [[index]] of about 500 large US companies, still fell more than half from late 2007 to early 2009.

## Example: one stock or twenty

You have $10,000 in one stock. The company reports bad news; the price falls 50%. You lose $5,000, half the account.

Now split the same $10,000 over 20 stocks from different industries, $500 each. The same news cuts one of them in half. You lose $250, 2.5% of the account.

A few dozen stocks from different industries remove most company-specific risk. An [[etf]], a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock, can hold a whole index: one purchase does this.

## Correlation: do they move together?

**Correlation** tells you how closely two prices move together: whether they tend to rise and fall on the same days. Two oil companies are closely correlated: both depend on the oil price. An oil company and a software company are less so. Different kinds of assets, such as stocks, [[bond|bonds]] and [[commodity|commodities]], often move differently, but not always.

Diversification works only with assets that are not closely correlated.

## Correlated positions add up

Here a trade means one position: a buy and the later sale that closes it.

A [[stop-loss]] sells automatically if the price falls to a level you chose. [[position-sizing|Position sizing]] picks the number of shares so that a hit stop costs a fixed part of the account, for example 1% of $10,000: $100. Say you hold three oil stocks; each stop risks $100. Then the oil price falls hard. All three stocks hit their stops in the same week: you lose $300, 3% of the account, on one idea.

Assets that move together act like one larger position, so their risks add up. Many traders limit the total risk of correlated positions.

## Watch out

- **In a crash, many assets fall together.** Correlations rise when markets panic, as in 2008.
- **Ten stocks can be one bet.** Check what drives each asset: industry, country, [[currency-risk|currency]]. Even a world stock fund is mostly in dollars.
- **A gap hits all correlated positions at once.** A [[gap]] can take all three past their stops: you lose more than $300.
- **Diversification makes one loss smaller; it does not stop losses.** It makes the gain from one good idea smaller too.
