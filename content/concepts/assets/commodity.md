A **commodity** is a raw material that is the same no matter who produces it, so traders deal in large standard amounts.

## Three groups

- **Energy**: crude oil, natural gas, petrol.
- **Metals**: gold and silver, and industrial metals such as copper.
- **Agriculture**: wheat, corn, coffee, cattle.

Prices, mostly in US dollars ([[currency-risk]]), move with supply and demand. Bad weather, wars or strikes can cut supply; a slower economy cuts demand.

## Spot price and futures price

The **spot price** is the price for delivery now. Most commodity trading uses [[futures]]: contracts to buy or sell at a set price on a set future date. Each expiry date has its own **futures price**, which nears the spot price at expiry.

Usually, a later futures price is higher than the spot price by about the **cost of carry**: the cost to hold the goods until that date (tanks or silos, insurance, and interest the money could earn instead).

- **Contango**: later futures cost more than the spot price. It is normal with enough supply. Gold is almost always in contango.
- **Backwardation**: later futures cost less than the spot price, as when supply is short and having the goods now is worth extra.

## Example: the roll cost

Oil is in contango:

| Contract | Price per barrel |
|----------|------------------|
| Spot | $70.00 |
| Next month | $70.50 |

A fund holds oil futures. Before expiry, it rolls: it sells the contract that ends soon, near the spot price of $70.00, and buys the next one at $70.50. If the spot price stays at $70.00, the new contract falls to it by expiry. The fund loses 50 cents per barrel, about 0.7%, while oil stood still. If contango stays like this for a year, twelve monthly rolls cost about 8%. Futures need only a deposit, so the fund's spare cash earns interest that pays part of the cost.

In backwardation, the roll gains instead.

## Ways to trade commodities

| Way | Good | Bad |
|-----|------|-----|
| Futures | Direct, low costs | Large contracts (smaller mini and micro versions exist); margin (a deposit, see futures); delivery dates |
| ETFs and ETCs | Easy, no margin | Roll cost if they hold futures; yearly fee |
| Shares of producers | Easy to buy, like any stock | Company risk and stock market moves too |
| CFDs | Small sizes | Leverage; overnight financing |

- An [[etf]] (a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock) or an **ETC** (exchange-traded commodity, common in Europe) is easy to buy. Most gold ETCs hold gold bars and follow the spot price, less a fee.
- Oil company shares follow oil only in part: company news and the [[stock]] market move them too. Several oil stocks are almost one bet ([[diversification]]).
- **[[cfd|CFDs]]** are contracts with a [[broker]] that pay the change in a price, common in the EU and UK but not offered to private traders in the US. You put down only a deposit, so gains and losses are large ([[leverage]]: in the EU and UK at most 20:1 for gold, 10:1 for other commodities). Most brokers charge [[fees-and-commissions|overnight financing]].

## Watch out

- **Prices can go to extremes.** In April 2020, a US oil futures contract near expiry fell below zero for one day, to about minus $37 a barrel: storage was full, so sellers paid buyers to take the oil.
- **Delivery is real.** Some contracts, like US oil (WTI), end in delivery. Close or roll before expiry, or your broker usually closes it.
