A **REIT** (real estate investment trust) is a company that owns property or property loans and pays most of its income to shareholders. Many REITs, including all the large well-known ones, are listed on an [[exchange]], and their shares trade like any other [[stock]], so one small trade buys you a part of many buildings.

## Two kinds

- **Equity REITs** own buildings and collect rent. Most keep to one sector: offices, flats, warehouses, data centres or shopping centres.
- **Mortgage REITs** own few or no buildings. They mostly lend money on property, or buy such loans, and earn interest.

## The payout rule

In return for paying little or no company tax, a REIT must pay out most of its income as a [[dividend]]. In the US, that is at least 90% of its taxable income each year; a UK REIT must pay out at least 90% of its rental profit. Because it keeps little profit, a REIT must borrow or sell new shares to buy more property.

## FFO: profit without depreciation

A company's profit subtracts **depreciation**: a yearly cost for the wear of its buildings. No money leaves the company for it, and well-kept buildings often keep their value. So REITs also report **FFO** (funds from operations): profit with property depreciation added back, and gains or losses from property sales left out. A REIT that pays out more than its FFO for years may have to cut its dividend.

## Example

A REIT trades at $40 and pays 50 cents each quarter (every three months): $2 a year. Its dividend yield (a year's dividends ÷ price) is $2 ÷ $40 = 5%.

Say rates rise and the price falls to $32; the dividend stays the same. The dividend yield is now $2 ÷ $32 = 6.25%, but you lost $8 per share (20%), more than a year of dividends.

## Why rising interest rates hurt

Income buyers compare a REIT's dividend yield with what new [[bond|bonds]] pay (their yield). When rates rise, new bonds pay more, so the REIT price falls until its dividend yield is attractive again. Its loan costs rise too. In 2022, US equity REITs lost about 25%.

## Many REITs at once

A REIT [[etf]], a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock, spreads your money over many REITs ([[diversification]]). But rising rates hit most of them together.

## Watch out

- **US REIT dividends are taxed more.** Most of the dividend is taxed like wages, not at the lower rate for most stock dividends, though many US holders can leave 20% of it out of their taxable income.
- **A high dividend yield can be a warning.** The price may have fallen as tenants left or debts grew; a cut often follows.
- **Borrowed money cuts both ways.** REITs buy much of their property with loans ([[leverage]]), so falling property values hit shareholders harder.
- **Unlisted REITs are hard to sell.** You may wait years to sell, and fees are often high.
