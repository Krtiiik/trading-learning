Valuation means judging whether a [[stock]] is expensive or cheap by comparing what the whole company costs with the profit it makes.

## Two numbers

- **Market value**, also called market cap: the share price times the number of shares.
- **Earnings**: the company's profit after all costs and taxes. US companies report it every quarter; for a year, add up the last four. Many European companies report fully only every half year; then add up the last two.

## The P/E ratio

The **P/E ratio** (price to earnings) is the share price divided by the profit per share for the last year. Market value divided by the whole year's profit gives the same number. So it is what you pay for $1 of yearly profit.

## Example

A company has 10,000,000 shares at $42, so its market value is $420 million. Over the last four quarters it made $30 million, or $3 per share.

- P/E: $42 / $3 = **14**.
- If it pays a [[dividend]] of 40 cents a quarter, that is $1.60 a year out of the $3 profit: about half.

A second company trades at $80 but made only $2 per share: a P/E of 40. Is it too expensive? Maybe not, if its profit grows fast. If its profit doubles twice, $2 becomes $8, and $80 is then only 10 times profit. But if the growth does not come, buyers who paid 40 times profit can lose a lot. A high P/E means buyers expect growth; a low one can mean they expect trouble.

## Using it

- **Compare like with like.** A fast-growing software company usually has a higher P/E than a slow, steady electricity company, so compare similar companies, or a company with its own past.
- **A whole [[index]] has a P/E too**: its companies' combined market value divided by their combined profit. [[buy-and-hold|Buy-and-hold]] investors in a broad index fund own cheap and expensive companies alike.
- **Earnings reports move prices.** A report much better or worse than expected can move the price sharply, often with a [[gap]] at the next open, because most companies report outside trading hours.
- **News can change what a company is worth.** After a bad earnings report, the old price may no longer fit the new profit. That is why [[mean-reversion]] often fails when a fall comes from news.

## Watch out

- **No profit, no P/E.** A company that lost money has no useful P/E; people then value it in other ways, such as its sales or expected future profit.
- **Profit can mislead.** A one-time sale of a factory can raise one year's profit and make the P/E look low.
- **Cheap can stay cheap.** A low P/E is not a buy signal and does not say when a price will move. It is one measure, not advice.
- **Past profit is not future profit.** Many apps also show a forward P/E, built on analysts' guesses of next year's profit.
