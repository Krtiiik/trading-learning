A **bond** is a loan that you give to a government or a company. The borrower pays you interest at fixed times. At the end, it pays back the amount it borrowed.

## The three numbers on a bond

- **Face value**: the amount the borrower pays back at the end, often $1,000 per bond in the US.
- **Coupon**: the yearly interest, in percent of the face value. For most bonds it is fixed. US government notes and bonds and UK gilts pay it in two halves each year; German government bonds pay once.
- **Maturity**: the date the loan ends and the face value comes back.

## Credit risk and ratings

**Credit risk** is the risk that the borrower does not pay. Bonds of large, rich governments are seen as the safest. **Company bonds** usually pay more, because a company is more likely to fail. Rating agencies such as S&P and Moody's grade borrowers with letters, from AAA (safest) down. **Investment grade** is BBB- or better. Below that is **high yield** (it pays more), also called **junk**, because more of these borrowers fail.

## Trading before maturity

You can sell a bond early. Most bonds trade between banks and large trading firms, not on an [[exchange]]; your [[broker]] gets a price from one of them. Prices are often given in percent of face value: 92 means $920 for a $1,000 bond.

## Why the price moves against interest rates

The coupon stays the same, but interest rates change. When rates rise, new bonds pay more, so an old bond with a lower coupon is worth less. Its price falls until its return matches the new bonds. When rates fall, its price rises.

The **yield** is the yearly return you get if you buy now and hold to maturity. It counts the coupons plus the gain or loss between your price and the face value. A lower price means a higher yield.

## Example

You buy a new 10-year bond for its face value, $1,000, with a 4% coupon. It pays $40 a year.

Then new 10-year bonds pay 5%. Your bond still pays $40, so its price falls to about $920. A buyer at that price gets $40 a year plus an $80 gain at maturity: a yield of about 5%. If rates fall to 3% instead, its price rises to about $1,085.

The same rise from 4% to 5% hurts more when more years are left:

| Years left | Price after the rise |
|------------|----------------------|
| 2 | about $980 |
| 10 | about $920 |
| 30 | about $850 |

This **interest rate risk** is larger for longer bonds.

## Watch out

- **A "safe" bond can lose money.** US government bonds with 20 or more years left lost about 30% of their price in 2022.
- **Bonds and stocks can fall together.** Both fell in 2022, so bonds do not always soften losses on your [[stock|stocks]]. See [[diversification]].
- **Most [[etf|bond ETFs]] never mature.** They usually sell bonds before they mature and buy new ones, so you get no fixed amount back; the price keeps moving with rates.
