A **bond** is a loan that you give to a government or a company. The borrower pays you interest at fixed times and, at the end, pays back the amount it borrowed.

## The three numbers on a bond

- **Face value**: the amount the borrower pays back at the end, often $1,000 per bond in the US. EU company bonds often come in minimum pieces of €1,000 or €100,000, too big for many small accounts.
- **Coupon**: the yearly interest, in percent of the face value. It is usually fixed.
- **Maturity**: the date the loan ends and the face value comes back.

## Credit risk and ratings

**Credit risk** is the risk that the borrower does not pay. Large, rich governments' bonds count as the safest. **Company bonds** usually pay more: companies fail more often. Rating agencies such as Moody's grade borrowers from AAA (safest) down. **Investment grade** is BBB- or better. Below that is **high yield**, or **junk**: it pays more, because more of these borrowers fail.

## Trading before maturity

You can sell a bond early. Most trade between banks and large trading firms, not on an [[exchange]]; your [[broker]] gets a price from one of them. Prices are often given in percent of face value: 92 means $920 for a $1,000 bond. Czech state savings bonds (Dluhopisy Republiky) are sold directly to people and not traded on any market; instead, you can ask for your money back early once a year.

## Why the price moves against interest rates

When interest rates rise, new bonds pay more, so an old bond with a lower coupon is worth less. Its price falls until its return matches the new bonds. When rates fall, its price rises.

The **yield** is the yearly return if you buy now and hold to maturity. It counts coupons plus the gain or loss from your price to face value. A lower price means a higher yield.

## Example

You buy a new 10-year bond for its face value, $1,000, with a 4% coupon. It pays $40 a year.

Then new 10-year bonds pay 5%. Your bond still pays $40, so its price falls to about $920. A buyer at that price gets $40 a year plus an $80 gain at maturity: a yield of about 5%. If rates fall to 3% instead, its price rises to about $1,085.

The same rise from 4% to 5% hurts more when more years are left (**interest rate risk**):

| Years left | Price after the rise |
|------------|----------------------|
| 2 | about $980 |
| 10 | about $920 |
| 30 | about $850 |

Rising rates hurt [[reit|REITs]] too.

## Watch out

- **A "safe" bond can lose money.** US government bonds with over 20 years left lost about 30% in 2022.
- **Bonds and stocks can fall together**, as in 2022, so bonds do not always soften losses on your [[stock|stocks]].
- **Most [[etf|bond ETFs]] never mature.** They keep replacing their bonds, so you get no fixed amount back.
- **A dollar bond pays in dollars.** For a CZK or EUR holder, a 10% dollar fall can erase years of coupons ([[currency-risk]]).
