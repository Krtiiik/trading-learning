Mean reversion is a [[strategy]] (fixed rules for when to buy and sell) built on one idea: a price that moved far from its usual level often comes back to it.

It buys a falling price and expects a turn back, the opposite of [[trend-following|trend following]], which buys a rising price and expects it to keep rising. Some traders also bet on falls from far above, with [[short-selling|short selling]]: selling borrowed shares, hoping to buy them back cheaper.

## The usual level, and how far is far

The usual level is often a [[moving-average]], the average of recent closing prices.

Ways to measure "far":

- **In percent**: 8% below the average.
- **In [[atr|ATRs]]**: 4 average daily moves below the average.
- **In standard deviations**: how far the closes usually are from their average. [[bollinger-bands|Bollinger bands]] show them.

[[rsi]] is another tool: a score from 0 to 100. Below 30 is often called oversold: recent falls were unusually strong compared with rises.

## Example

A [[stock]] has moved between about $45 and $55 for months. Its 20-day average is $50, and its ATR is $1. The [[entry-and-exit-rules]]:

- **Enter**: buy on a close 4 ATRs or more below the average. It closes at $46, near the bottom of its trading range: you buy.
- **Exit**: sell on the first close at or above the average (about $50 today).
- **[[stop-loss|Stop loss]]**: an order that sells automatically if the price falls to $42, 4 ATRs below the buy price. That is wider than a stop just below the support zone ($43.50, see [[support-and-resistance]]): more room, but more risk per share.

The risk is $46 − $42 = $4 per share, and the reward about $50 − $46 = $4: a [[risk-reward]] of 1 to 1, so you must win more than half your trades to break even. In practice the win is often smaller: falling closes pull the average, and so the exit, down toward your buy price.

## When it works, and when it fails

It works best in a sideways market, where the price swings between roughly the same levels (see [[trend]]). Dips near the lower level (support) tend to turn back.

It fails when the price keeps falling in a new downtrend. Traders call this **catching a falling knife**.

## What it feels like

Expect many small wins and a few large losses, the reverse of trend following. Large losses come from a too-wide stop, a [[gap]] through the stop, or no stop at all. The [[win-rate-and-expectancy|win rate]] (the share of trades that end in a gain) is often high, but one loss can cancel several wins.

## Watch out

- **The price can stay far away longer than you can wait.**
- **Always use a stop.** Without one, a new downtrend can take away months of small gains.
- **News can change the usual level.** After a bad profit report, a $50 stock may now be worth $40, and the old average is out of date.
