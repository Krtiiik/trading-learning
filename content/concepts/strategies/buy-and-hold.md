Buy and hold is a [[strategy]] where you buy an asset and keep it for many years. You do not sell on short rises or falls or try to time your trades.

## How it works

- **Few sales.** You buy, then wait. Many people also buy a little more each month.
- **Low costs.** Rare selling keeps [[fees-and-commissions|fees]] and [[spread]] costs low.
- **Low tax.** Long holds are taxed less: usually tax-free after 3 years in Czechia, a lower rate after one year in the US ([[taxes-on-trading]]).
- **No timing.** The only rule is to keep holding: there is no exit signal and usually no [[stop-loss|stop loss]].

## Compounding

[[compounding|Compounding]] means that your gains also earn gains, so long holds grow fastest at the end. Example, not a forecast: $10,000 that grows 7% a year becomes about $76,000 after 30 years. Reinvested [[dividend|dividends]] buy more shares, which pay dividends too.

## Time in the market, not timing the market

Timing the market means selling before falls and buying back before rises. It is hard: the best days often come soon after the worst, when [[trading-psychology|fear]] is high. A J.P. Morgan study looked at the S&P 500, a US stock [[index]], from 2005 to 2024. Missing only its 10 best days left an investor with less than half as much money.

## Why a broad index fund

Most buy-and-hold investors buy an [[etf]] that follows a broad index, not one [[stock]]. One company can fail; a fund with hundreds of companies removes most of that risk ([[diversification]]). Broad ETFs often have an expense ratio of only 0.03% to 0.20% a year. This fee compounds too: over 30 years at 7%, $10,000 grows to about $74,600 at 0.07%, but only about $61,600 at 0.75%.

In a mix of stocks and [[bond|bonds]], [[rebalancing]] trades back to your chosen mix as prices shift it.

## Watch out

- **Falls can be deep and long.** The S&P 500 fell about 57% from October 2007 to March 2009, which needs a gain of about 133% to recover (see [[drawdown]]). The Nasdaq Composite fell about 78% from 2000 to 2002 and regained its 2000 high only in 2015.
- **You must hold through the falls.** Selling near the bottom locks in the loss. Keep money you need soon out of stocks.
- **Past returns do not promise future ones.** Japan's Nikkei 225 needed about 34 years, 1989 to 2024, to regain its high. These figures are index price levels in each index's own currency (dollars for the US ones, yen for the Nikkei): with dividends reinvested, recovery came sooner, and a CZK or EUR holder's result differed (see [[currency-risk]]).
