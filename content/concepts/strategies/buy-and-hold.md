Buy and hold is a [[strategy]] where you buy an asset and keep it for many years. You do not sell on short rises or falls, and you do not try to time your trades.

## How it works

- **Few sales.** You buy, then wait. Many people also buy a little more each month.
- **Low costs.** Rare selling and small regular buys keep [[fees-and-commissions|fees]] and [[spread]] costs low.
- **No timing.** The only rule is to keep holding: there is no exit signal and usually no [[stop-loss|stop loss]].

## Compounding

Compounding means that your gains also earn gains. Example, not a forecast: $10,000 grows 7% every year. In year 1 it earns $700. In year 2, $10,700 earns $749: the $700 earns too. After 30 years: about $76,000. Taking the gain out each year leaves only $10,000 + 30 × $700 = $31,000. Real returns vary, and some years are losses.

When you reinvest each [[dividend]] (money a company pays for each share you own) to buy more shares, the new shares pay dividends too.

## Time in the market, not timing the market

Timing the market means selling before falls and buying back before rises. It is hard: the best days often come soon after the worst days, when fear is high. A J.P. Morgan study looked at the S&P 500, an [[index]] (a number that tracks the combined price of a basket of stocks), over 20 years (2005 to 2024). An investor who was out of the market on only its 10 best days ended with less than half as much money.

## Why a broad index fund

Most buy-and-hold investors buy an [[etf]] (a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock) that follows a broad index, not one [[stock]]. One company can fail; a fund with hundreds of companies removes most of this company-specific risk ([[diversification]]). Broad ETFs often have an expense ratio of only 0.03% to 0.20% a year. This fee compounds too: over the same 30 years at 7%, $10,000 grows to about $74,600 at 0.07%, but only about $61,600 at 0.75%.

If you hold a mix, such as stocks and [[bond|bonds]] (loans to governments or companies), prices shift the mix over the years. [[rebalancing|Rebalancing]] trades back to the mix you chose.

## Watch out

- **Falls can be deep and long.** The S&P 500 fell about 57% from October 2007 to March 2009, which needs a gain of about 133% to recover (see [[drawdown]]). The Nasdaq Composite index fell about 78% from 2000 to 2002 and regained its 2000 high only in 2015, 15 years later.
- **One stock is not the market.** It can fail for good.
- **You must hold through the falls.** Selling near the bottom locks in the loss. Keep money you need soon out of stocks.
- **Past returns do not promise future ones.** Japan's Nikkei 225 needed about 34 years, from 1989 to 2024, to get back to its high. All these figures are price levels: with dividends reinvested, recovery came sooner.
