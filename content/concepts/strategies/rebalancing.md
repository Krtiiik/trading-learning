Rebalancing means trading back to the mix of assets you chose, after price moves have changed it. You sell some of what grew too large and buy more of what became too small.

## Target mix and drift

Your **target mix** (also called **asset allocation**) is the share of your money you want in each kind of asset. A common example is 60% [[stock|stocks]] and 40% [[bond|bonds]] (a 60/40 mix). Bonds are loans to governments or companies. Stocks usually [[volatility|swing more]], so more stocks means more risk.

Prices do not move together, so the mix changes by itself: **drift**.

In a [[momentum]] [[strategy]], the target mix is equal money in each stock held, so rebalancing there means moving money to make them equal again.

## Example

You put $10,000 into a 60/40 mix: $6,000 in stocks and $4,000 in bonds.

Stocks rise 30%; bonds stay flat. Stocks are now $7,800, bonds $4,000, the total $11,800. Stocks are about 66% ($7,800 / $11,800).

60% of $11,800 is $7,080. You sell $720 of stocks and buy $720 of bonds. That leaves $7,080 in stocks and $4,720 in bonds: 60/40 again.

## When to rebalance

- **Calendar rebalancing**: on a fixed date, for example once a year.
- **Threshold rebalancing**: only when a part is a set distance from the target mix, for example 5 percentage points (stocks above 65% or below 55%). In the example, stocks at 66% pass that line, so this rule acts now.

## With new money instead of selling

Put regular new money into the part that is too small. In the example, $7,800 of stocks is 60% of $13,000, so bonds need $5,200: $1,200 of new money, all into bonds, gives 60/40 again with no sale.

## What it does

- **It keeps the mix, and so roughly the risk, that you chose.** Without it, a 60/40 mix usually drifts toward stocks, because they tend to rise more over the years, and the next crash hits harder (see [[drawdown]]).
- **It makes you sell what rose the most and buy what fell behind.** A fixed rule, as in any strategy, means fear and hope do not decide.

## Watch out

- **Each sale can cost money.** You pay [[fees-and-commissions|fees]] and the [[spread]] (the gap between the best price buyers offer and the best price sellers want). In many countries, a sale at a gain also means tax. New money avoids this, and so do tax-advantaged accounts (special retirement or savings accounts in some countries): selling inside them causes no tax at that time.
- **Do not rebalance too often.** Small drifts often reverse by themselves; trading on each one adds costs but little control.
- **It does not promise a higher return.** In a long rise in stocks, it keeps selling the winner, so a mix you never rebalance can end higher.
- **It means buying what is falling.** In a crash, the rule says to buy more stocks while they drop. That is hard, and they can keep falling.
