**Short selling** (going short) is a way to gain when a price falls.

## The steps

1. **Borrow.** Your [[broker]] lends you shares.
2. **Sell.** You sell them at the market price; the broker holds the cash as security.
3. **Buy back.** You buy the same number of shares to close.
4. **Return.** The shares go back.

While the position is open, you pay:

- **A borrow fee**: daily interest on the borrowed shares' value, small for most large [[stock|stocks]] but many percent a year for shares few people lend.
- **Any [[dividend]]**: if the company pays one, you pay the same amount to the lender.

## Example

You have a $10,000 account and risk 1%: $100. You short 25 shares at $42; the sale brings in $1,050. Your [[stop-loss]] is a buy stop at $46, which loses 25 × $4 = $100.

| Price when you buy back | Cost to buy back | Result |
|-------------------------|------------------|--------|
| $34 | $850 | $200 gain |
| $46 (the stop) | $1,150 | $100 loss |
| $50 (after a [[gap]], see below) | $1,250 | $200 loss |

The stop does not guarantee its price. If the stock closes at $44 and opens at $50 after good news, your stop buys around $50: $200 lost, not $100.

## US rules

- Shorting needs a margin account (an account where the broker can lend to you; see [[leverage]]).
- Under **SEC Rule 201**, after a listed stock falls 10% or more in a day, short sales for the rest of that day and the next are allowed only above the national best [[bid-and-ask|bid]] (the highest buyer's price across US markets).

## EU rules

- The EU Short Selling Regulation bans naked short selling: selling shares you have not borrowed or arranged to borrow.
- Regulators can ban shorting for a time; several did in March 2020.
- A net short position of 0.1% or more of a company's shares must be reported to the regulator (0.5% is made public). Private traders rarely reach this, except in small companies.

## Other ways to go short

Most EU brokers for private traders do not offer stock shorting. The main route is [[cfd|CFDs]] (contracts with a broker that pay the change in a price), which are common in the EU and UK but not offered to private traders in the US. You borrow no shares but usually pay [[fees-and-commissions|overnight financing]] for each night held.

An inverse [[etf]] (a fund that holds many assets and trades like one stock) aims for the opposite of an [[index]]'s move, and you buy it in a normal account. It aims at each day's move, so over weeks its result often differs from the opposite of the index.

## Watch out

- **The possible loss has no limit.** A price can rise without limit but fall only to zero, so you can gain at most the $1,050, less fees and dividends.
- **A short squeeze.** When a heavily shorted stock rises, short sellers buy back to cut losses, pushing it higher.
- **The lender can recall the shares.** If no other shares are found, the broker can close your position without asking.
- **A rising price can cause a [[leverage|margin call]]**: the broker asks you to add money or close.
