An **exchange** is a marketplace where buyers and sellers are matched. Examples are the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq in the US, the London Stock Exchange, and in the EU Xetra (Frankfurt), Euronext and the Prague Stock Exchange.

## What an exchange does

- **It keeps the order book.** This is the list of orders waiting to be filled (carried out).
- **It matches orders.** Its **matching engine** checks each new order against the book. On most stock exchanges, the best price fills first (the lowest sell price for a buyer, the highest buy price for a seller); at the same price, the earlier order fills first.
- **It lists assets.** A company must meet the exchange's rules before its [[stock]] is listed there. In the US and Europe, other exchanges can then trade it too.
- **It publishes every trade made on it.** In the US, trades away from exchanges are reported too, and most charts and [[volume]] (how much was traded) figures include them.
- **It sets its hours.** See [[trading-hours]].

## Exchange and broker

Only members of the exchange, such as brokers and large trading firms, can send it orders directly. You open an account with a [[broker]], and the broker sends your order to an exchange, or another market, for you. The broker, not the exchange, holds your money and shares.

## Example

You place a market buy (trade now, see [[order-types]]) for 100 shares. The order book shows sellers waiting at $50.02 (100 shares) and $50.03 (200 shares). The lower, $50.02, is the ask: the lowest price a seller wants right now (see [[bid-and-ask]]).

1. Your broker sends the order to the exchange.
2. The matching engine fills it: 100 shares at $50.02, so you pay $5,002.
3. No shares wait at $50.02 any more, so the new ask is $50.03.
4. The exchange reports the trade: the last price is now $50.02, and volume grows by 100.

## Markets with no central exchange

- **[[forex-pair|Forex]].** Most forex trades between many banks and brokers, not on an exchange. Your broker fills your order itself or passes it to a bank or large trading firm.
- **[[cryptocurrency|Crypto]].** Coins trade on many exchanges, each with its own order book. A crypto exchange is often broker and exchange at once: it holds your account and coins, and matches your orders itself.

## Watch out

- **Your order may not reach an exchange.** In the US, large trading firms pay brokers for many small orders from private traders, and fill them directly, not on an exchange ([[fees-and-commissions|payment for order flow]]).
- **One stock can trade in many places.** In the US and Europe, a stock often trades on several exchanges, so one exchange's order book shows only part of its orders.
- **A crypto exchange holds your coins.** If it fails, you can lose them. In many countries, the law makes stock brokers keep clients' shares apart from their own; a crypto exchange often has no such duty.
