An **exchange** is a marketplace where buyers and sellers are matched. Examples are the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq in the US, and the London Stock Exchange.

## What an exchange does

- **It keeps the order book.** This is the list of orders waiting to be filled (carried out).
- **It matches orders.** Its **matching engine** checks each new order against the book. On most stock exchanges, the best price fills first (the lowest sell price for a buyer, the highest buy price for a seller); at the same price, the earlier order fills first.
- **It lists assets.** A company must meet the exchange's rules before its [[stock]] is listed there. Other exchanges can then trade it too.
- **It publishes every trade made on it.** Trades made away from exchanges must be reported too; charts and [[volume]] (how much was traded) use all of them.
- **It sets its hours.** See [[trading-hours]].

## Exchange and broker

Only firms that are members of the exchange, such as brokers and large trading firms, can send orders to it. You open an account with a [[broker]], and the broker sends your order to an exchange, or another place where orders are matched, for you. The broker holds your money and shares; the exchange never holds your account.

## Example

You place a market buy (trade now, see [[order-types]]) for 100 shares. The order book shows sellers waiting at $50.02 (100 shares) and $50.03 (200 shares). The lowest of these, $50.02, is the ask: the cheapest price a seller wants right now (see [[bid-and-ask]]).

1. Your broker sends the order to the exchange.
2. The matching engine fills it: 100 shares at $50.02, so you pay $5,002.
3. No shares wait at $50.02 any more, so the new ask is $50.03.
4. The exchange reports the trade: the last price is now $50.02, and volume grows by 100.

## Markets with no central exchange

- **[[forex-pair|Forex]].** Most forex trades between many banks and brokers, not on an exchange. Your forex broker fills your order itself or passes it to a bank or another large trading firm.
- **[[cryptocurrency|Crypto]].** Coins trade on many exchanges, each with its own order book. A crypto exchange is often broker and exchange at once: it holds your account and coins, and matches your orders itself.

## Watch out

- **Your order may not reach an exchange.** In the US, many small orders from private traders go to large trading firms that trade with you directly, not on an exchange.
- **One stock can trade in many places.** In the US and Europe, a stock often trades on several exchanges, so one exchange's order book shows only part of its orders.
- **A crypto exchange holds your coins.** If it fails, you can lose them. In many countries, the law makes stock brokers keep clients' shares apart from their own; a crypto exchange often has no such duty.
