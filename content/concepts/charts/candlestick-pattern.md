A candlestick pattern is a shape made by one, two or three [[candle|candles]] in a row. Traders give each common shape a name and read it as a hint about the next few candles, often a sign that a rise or a fall is about to turn.

Each pattern is built from the body (the thick part, from the open to the close: the first and last price of the period, see [[ohlc]]) and the [[wick|wicks]] (the thin lines up to the high and down to the low). An up candle (usually green) closed above its own open; a down candle (usually red) closed below it. The candle before does not matter.

## Common patterns

| Pattern | Candles | Shape | What it is said to show |
|---------|---------|-------|-------------------------|
| Doji | 1 | Open and close almost equal, so the body is a thin line | Buyers and sellers are in balance |
| Hammer | 1 | Small body near the top, lower wick at least twice the body, little or no upper wick, after a fall | Sellers pushed the price down, but buyers pushed it back up |
| Shooting star | 1 | Small body near the bottom, upper wick at least twice the body, little or no lower wick, after a rise | Buyers pushed the price up, but sellers pushed it back down |
| Bullish engulfing (bullish: hinting at a rise) | 2 | After a fall: a down candle, then an up candle whose body covers the whole first body | Buyers took control |
| Bearish engulfing (bearish: hinting at a fall) | 2 | After a rise: an up candle, then a down candle whose body covers the whole first body | Sellers took control |
| Morning star | 3 | After a fall: a long down candle, a small-body candle that sits lower, then a long up candle that closes above the middle of the first body | A fall may be ending |

A hammer or a doji can be either colour.

## Context matters

A hammer counts only after a fall: the same shape after a rise has a different name (a hanging man) and a different reading. A hammer means more when its low touches a [[support-and-resistance|support]] level, a price where earlier falls stopped. When the price moves sideways, with no clear [[trend]] before the pattern, it says little.

## Wait for the next candle

Many traders wait for confirmation: the next candle must move the way the pattern suggests. After a hammer, some traders want a close above the hammer's close; stricter traders want a close above its high.

## Example

A [[stock]] falls for several days. Then one day it opens at $45.20, drops to $43, climbs back to a high of $45.50 and closes at $45.40.

- The body runs from $45.20 to $45.40: 20 cents tall. The candle is an up candle (usually green).
- The lower wick runs from $45.20 down to $43: $2.20, eleven times the body.
- The upper wick is only 10 cents.

That is a hammer. The next day the stock closes at $46.10, above the hammer's high of $45.50, so the pattern is confirmed. You buy near the close, at about $46.10, and put a [[stop-loss]] a little below the hammer's low, at $42.80. If the price falls below $43, the buyers who pushed it back up have failed. The planned risk is $3.30 per share; a gap past the stop can make it more.

## Watch out

- **A pattern alone is a weak signal.** Many hammers come before more falls. Use a pattern with other evidence, such as a support level or the trend, and test your rules on past data with a [[backtest]] before you trust them.
- **Definitions differ.** Books and apps do not agree on how long a wick must be or how small a body must be. Write down your exact rule.
- **The newest candle is not finished.** A hammer can turn into a different shape before its period ends.
- **Patterns depend on the [[timeframe]]**, how much time each candle covers. A clear hammer on a 5-minute chart may span only a few cents and disappear inside one daily candle.
