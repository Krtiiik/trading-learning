The timeframe is how much time each [[candle]] or bar on a [[price-chart]] covers. That time is one period. On a daily chart, each candle is one day (one trading day for stocks). On an hourly chart, each candle is one hour.

## Common timeframes

Common choices:

- **Minutes**: 1-minute, 5-minute, 15-minute, 30-minute.
- **Hours**: 1-hour, 4-hour.
- **Days and longer**: daily, weekly, monthly.

Traders who open and close positions within one day often use 1-minute to 1-hour charts. People who hold for weeks or months use daily and weekly charts.

## Longer candles come from shorter ones

A longer candle combines shorter ones: the open (first price) of the first, the highest high, the lowest low, and the close (last price) of the last. The [[ohlc]] page shows three hourly candles that combine into one 3-hour candle. So a new timeframe does not change the price history, only how it is grouped.

## Example

A US [[stock]] rises from $50 to $53 in one week. The exchange is open 6.5 hours a day (its regular [[trading-hours|trading hours]], 9:30 to 16:00 New York time).

- On a **weekly** chart, the week is one candle. It is an up candle (usually green) because it closed higher than it opened.
- On a **daily** chart, it is five candles, perhaps three up and two down.
- On a **5-minute** chart, a normal week is 390 candles (78 a day, counting only the regular session), full of small rises and falls.

The same price history looks calm on the weekly chart and busy on the 5-minute chart.

## Indicators count periods

An [[indicator]], a number calculated from past prices or [[volume]], counts periods, not days. That number is its length (see [[moving-average]]). A 20-period moving average on a daily chart covers 20 trading days, about four weeks. That is why people call it a "20-day" average. On an hourly chart, the same setting covers 20 hours; on a 5-minute chart, only 100 minutes.

## Checking a longer timeframe

Many traders first check a longer timeframe for the [[trend]], then a shorter one to choose when to act: the weekly chart for the direction, then the daily chart to choose the day. The same rule gives different signals on different timeframes, so a [[strategy]] (fixed rules for when to buy and sell) states its timeframe.

## Watch out

- **A trend on one timeframe can be noise on another.** Noise is small, random moves with no lasting direction. A downtrend on an hourly chart can be a short dip inside an uptrend on the weekly chart. Always say which timeframe you mean.
- **Short timeframes show more noise and cost more.** They usually mean more trades: you pay part of the [[spread]] on most trades, and [[fees-and-commissions|fees]] too if your broker charges them.
- **The newest candle is not finished.** On a weekly chart, its close is not final for days.
