A chart pattern is a shape that many [[candle|candles]] make together on a [[price-chart]], often over weeks or months. Traders give the common shapes names and read each one as a hint: the [[trend]] will turn, or it will continue.

A [[candlestick-pattern]] uses one to three candles. A chart pattern is larger: you draw it through [[peak-and-trough|peaks and troughs]], the local high and low points of the price.

## Reversal and continuation

- A **reversal pattern** hints that the trend will turn: a rise becomes a fall, or a fall becomes a rise.
- A **continuation pattern** hints that the trend will continue after a short pause.

## Common patterns

| Pattern | Type | Shape |
|---------|------|-------|
| Double top | Reversal, after a rise | Two peaks at about the same price, with a trough between them |
| Double bottom | Reversal, after a fall | Two troughs at about the same price, with a peak between them |
| Head and shoulders | Reversal, after a rise | Three peaks: the middle one (the head) is the highest, the two others (the shoulders) are about equal |
| Inverse head and shoulders | Reversal, after a fall | The same shape upside down: three troughs, the middle one the lowest |
| Triangle | Usually continuation, but it can break either way | A line along the peaks and a line along the troughs come together, so the swings get smaller |
| Flag | Continuation | After a fast, steep move (the pole), a short, narrow pause that slopes a little against the pole's direction |

## The neckline

The neckline is the line that the price must break to complete the pattern. In a double top, it is the level of the trough between the two peaks. In a head and shoulders, it joins the two troughs, one on each side of the head, so it can slope. Triangles and flags use their own lines.

Most traders want a close (the last price of the period) beyond the neckline, not only a [[wick]] through it; [[breakout]] gives stricter rules. After the break, the price often comes back to the neckline (a retest). A top's neckline was [[support-and-resistance|support]] and can become resistance; a bottom's was resistance and can become support.

## The measured-move target

The usual [[risk-reward|target]] uses the pattern's height: the distance from the neckline to the point of the pattern farthest from it. Measure the same distance again from the point where the price broke the neckline, in the direction of the break. For a triangle, use its height at the widest part, where it starts; for a flag, the length of the pole.

## Example

A [[stock]] (not the $45–$55 one on other pages) rises, then:

| Turning point | Price  |
|---------------|--------|
| Peak          | $60.00 |
| Trough        | $54.00 |
| Peak          | $59.80 |

The peaks are 20 cents apart: a possible double top, with the neckline at $54.

Weeks later, a close at $53.40, below $54, confirms the pattern. The height is $60 − $54 = $6, so the target is $54 − $6 = $48. A trader who expects the fall may use [[short-selling]] (selling borrowed shares, hoping to buy them back cheaper), with $48 as the target and a [[stop-loss|buy stop]] just above the higher peak, at $60.50. The risk ($7.10) is larger than the reward ($5.40): under 1 to 1, so many traders skip this trade or wait for a retest to sell higher.

## Watch out

- **Shapes are clear only in hindsight.** While a double top forms, you cannot know that $59.80 is a peak, and many possible double tops never break the neckline.
- **People see them differently.** Two traders can draw different lines through the same chart. Write down exact rules: how close the peaks must be, and what counts as a break.
- **Many patterns fail.** The price can break the neckline and come back: a false breakout. The target is only a rough guide.
- **Test first.** Run your exact rules on past data with a [[backtest]].
