A price chart is a picture of how the price of something, such as a [[stock]], moved over time. It turns a list of prices into a shape you can read quickly.

## The two axes

- The **horizontal axis** is time, oldest on the left, newest on the right.
- The **vertical axis** is price.

A line climbing to the right means the price rose.

## One mark per period

A chart does not show every trade (each time a buyer and seller agree on a price). Most charts cut time into equal periods, such as one day or one hour, and draw one mark per period. That period is the chart's [[timeframe]].

## Example

A stock's close (its last price of the trading day) over five days:

| Day | Close |
|-----|-------|
| Mon | $50   |
| Tue | $52   |
| Wed | $51   |
| Thu | $54   |
| Fri | $56   |

Put a dot for each close, Monday on the left and Friday on the right, and join the dots: the line climbs from $50 to $56, with one dip on Wednesday. The week rose $6, or 12%.

## Line or candles

- A **line chart** joins only the closing prices, as in the example. It shows the direction well.
- A **candle chart** draws one [[candle]] per period. Each candle shows the first, highest, lowest and last price of the period: the open, high, low and close ([[ohlc]]).

Green usually means the candle closed higher than it opened, and red means it closed lower. Some apps, especially in China and Japan, use the opposite, so check yours.

Simple trading apps often start with a line chart; apps made for reading charts usually start with candles. See [[line-vs-candle-chart]].

## What else is on a chart

Many charts have a row of bars under the price line or candles that shows [[volume]]: how many shares (or units) were bought and sold in each period. Traders also add [[indicator|indicators]], numbers calculated from past prices or volume, such as a [[moving-average]].

## What you read from it

- The [[trend]]: up, down or sideways overall?
- The [[peak-and-trough|peaks and troughs]] where the price changed direction.
- [[support-and-resistance|Support and resistance]]: levels where falls (support) or rises (resistance) stopped more than once.

## Watch out

- **The timeframe changes the look.** The same price history looks calm on a weekly chart and busy on a 5-minute chart.
- **Check the numbers on the price axis.** Most charts start the axis just below the lowest price and end it just above the highest, so a small move looks big. With an axis from $49 to $57, the example's 12% rise fills most of the picture. From $0 to $60, it looks like a gentle slope.
- **A chart shows only the past.** It tells you what the price did, not what it will do next.
