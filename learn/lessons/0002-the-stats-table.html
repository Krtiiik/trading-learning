Lesson 2 · Measuring performance

Five numbers: CAGR, volatility, Sharpe, drawdown, worst day

After this lesson you can compute every number in the notebook's “Annualized statistics” table by hand, and say what each one hides.

All five come from the same two series in notebooks/explore_bars.py:

prices  = load_panel(picker.value).dropna()   # adj_close, one column per symbol
returns = prices.pct_change().dropna()        # daily total returns

Two details matter before any formula:

  • .dropna() keeps only dates where every picked symbol has data. GLD starts in November 2004, so picking SPY together with GLD cuts SPY's 1993–2004 history away. The same symbol gets different statistics depending on its neighbours in the picker.
  • A year is 252 sessions, the usual count of US trading days. The notebook converts sessions into years with len(returns) / 252.

The worked numbers below are from your store: SPY, TLT and GLD from 2004-11-18 to 2026-11-16, 5,533 sessions.

1 · CAGR: how fast did it grow?

Compound annual growth rate: the single constant yearly rate that would turn the starting price into the ending price over the same number of years.

CAGR = (Pend / Pstart)1 / years − 1

It looks only at the two endpoints. A path that crashed 50% and recovered and a perfectly smooth path get the same CAGR. That is what the next numbers are for.

2 · Volatility: how bumpy was the ride?

The standard deviation of daily returns, scaled to a year:

volatility = std(daily returns) × √252

Why √252 and not 252? If daily returns are independent, their variances add up over a year: 252 days give 252× the variance, so the standard deviation grows by √252 ≈ 15.9. SPY's daily standard deviation is about 1.19%, so its annual volatility is about 18.8%.

The √252 rule assumes days are independent. When returns are autocorrelated it is wrong, sometimes badly; see Lo, “The Statistics of Sharpe Ratios” (2002). For liquid ETFs it is a fine approximation.

Volatility counts upside moves as “risk” exactly like downside ones. It measures bumpiness, not loss.

3 · Sharpe ratio: return per unit of bumpiness

Sharpe = mean(daily returns) / std(daily returns) × √252

This equals annualized mean return (mean × 252) divided by annualized volatility (std × √252). SPY: 11.94% / 18.84% ≈ 0.63. It lets you compare a calm asset with a wild one on one scale.

Sharpe's own definition, in his 1994 paper, uses the return in excess of a benchmark, normally the risk-free rate (T-bills). The notebook sets that rate to zero (“rf=0”), which flatters every asset when cash pays something. Even a modest 2% cash rate pulls SPY's to (11.9 − 2) / 18.8 ≈ 0.53.

Why is the 11.94% mean higher than the 10.69% CAGR? Bumpiness costs compounding: lose 10% then gain 10% and you are at 0.99, not 1. The drag is about half the variance, 0.188² / 2 ≈ 1.8%, exact when CAGR is written as a log rate: ln(1.1069) = 10.2% = 11.9% − 1.8%. That is why Sharpe uses the arithmetic mean but you should quote CAGR as “what you earned”.

4 · Max drawdown: the worst fall from a peak

The drawdown on any day is how far the price sits below the highest price seen so far:

drawdown = price / running maximum − 1   ·   max drawdown = its minimum

In pandas that is prices / prices.cummax() - 1, and the table shows .min(). It is 0 at every new high and negative in between; the notebook's “Drawdown from previous peak” chart plots the whole series. SPY's −55.19% is the trough of 9 March 2009, measured from the October 2007 peak.

Drawdowns are asymmetric. After falling 55% you need +123% to get back to the peak (1 / 0.45 − 1). This is the number that tells you whether you could have stomached holding something.

Two limits: it uses daily closes, so the intraday lows were deeper; and it uses adj_close, so dividends received during the fall cushion it.

5 · Worst day

Simply returns.min(): the single largest one-session loss. SPY: −10.94% on 16 March 2020. GLD: −10.27% on 30 January 2026, the precious-metals crash the architecture notes mention. It is one observation, so it says little about what is likely, but a lot about what is possible.

Practice

Do these without scrolling up. A calculator is fine; looking up the formula is not.

1. A fund goes from 100 to 200 in exactly 10 years. What is its CAGR? (percent, one decimal)

(200 / 100)1/10 − 1 = 20.1 − 1 ≈ 7.2%. Not 10%: that would be the simple average, which ignores compounding.

2. Daily returns have a standard deviation of 1.0%. What is the annualized volatility? (percent)

1.0% × √252 ≈ 1.0% × 15.87 = 15.9%. Variances add over independent days, so the standard deviation scales with the square root of time.

3. Closing prices over five sessions: 100, 120, 90, 130, 104. What is the max drawdown? (percent, negative)

Running maximum: 100, 120, 120, 130, 130. Drawdowns: 0, 0, −25%, 0, −20%. The minimum is −25% (120 → 90). The later fall from 130 to 104 is smaller.

4. You add GLD to a picker that held only SPY. SPY's CAGR in the table changes. Why?

.dropna() keeps only dates where every picked symbol has a price. GLD's first bar is 2004-11-18, so SPY loses 1993–2004 and its endpoints move.

5. Two funds have the same CAGR. Fund A has twice the volatility of fund B. What follows?

Same growth with more bumpiness means less return per unit of volatility. (Strictly, Sharpe uses the arithmetic mean, which is a bit higher for the bumpier fund, hence “most likely”.) Drawdown and worst day are path details that volatility does not pin down.

6. A position falls 50% from its peak. What gain from the trough gets it back to the peak? (percent)

1 / 0.5 − 1 = 100%. Losses and the gains needed to repair them are not symmetric.

Try it on your own data 
import numpy as np
from trading.data import load_panel
prices = load_panel(["SPY"]).dropna()        # SPY alone: history from 1993
r = prices.pct_change().dropna()
years = len(r) / 252
(prices.iloc[-1] / prices.iloc[0]) ** (1 / years) - 1   # CAGR, compare with the table above
(prices / prices.cummax() - 1).idxmin()                  # date of the deepest drawdown
